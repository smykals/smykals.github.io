How I Lead

Leadership isn't one-size-fits-all. Some technicians needed a detailed walkthrough; others needed me to get out of the way. I adjusted to the person while keeping the same expectations for everyone.

  • Lead by Example

    I kept doing network work the whole time, so I never asked a technician to do something I wasn't doing myself.

  • Autonomy, With Accountability

    Technicians owned their campuses and their tickets. I tracked the results and stepped in when something slipped.

  • Approachable and Available

    A technician alone on a site with a problem they can't solve needs to be able to reach someone. I made sure that was me.

  • Recognize in Public, Correct in Private

    Good work got called out in front of the team. Problems were handled one on one.

  • Decide on Evidence and Impact

    Priorities came from how many people were affected and what the data showed, not from who was loudest.

  • Build Independent Problem-Solvers

    The goal was technicians who could handle the next problem without calling me.

  • Understand Mistakes Before Assigning Blame

    Most mistakes trace back to a gap in training, tools or process. I looked for that first.

  • Coach, Then Document

    Coaching fixes most problems. Documentation keeps the ones that don't from repeating.

Technician onboarding and development lifecycle.

When a new technician started, I traveled to their campus and spent about a week working alongside them. I introduced them to the campus and its people, showed them the job on real equipment, and taught hands-on rather than from slides.

After that week, weekly training sessions covered new material and whatever questions had come up. I used the company's training resources, and when an explanation wasn't landing, I wrote my own, simpler documentation. As technicians grew, I built individual development plans, assigned them more advanced technical work, recognized their progress in front of the team, and mentored the ones who wanted to move toward leadership.

Featured Case A: Move-In Workforce Planning

Move-in is the busiest week of the year for a campus network. Students arrive all at once, many connecting devices to the network for the first time. I was responsible for making sure there were enough technicians across fifteen campuses to handle it.

I forecast where demand would land, built the coverage schedules, arranged travel and temporary assignments, moved available technicians to where they were needed most, handled absences as they happened, and kept campus representatives informed.

1. Put help where first-time connections are

Freshmen and international students were most likely to need help getting connected for the first time, so they got priority.

2. Scale to campus size and demand

Larger and busier campuses got additional technicians beyond their regular coverage.

3. Keep everyone within reach

Where a campus had no dedicated on-site technician, I aimed to keep someone within about one to two hours of it.

Move-in staffing allocation: demand-based staffing and geographic resource planning. Headcounts per campus varied year to year and aren't listed here.

Featured Case B: Winter Fiber Outages

Underground fiber between apartment buildings on one campus had been installed in a way that let water into the vault enclosures. When temperatures dropped below freezing, optical signal levels fell to around -29 dBm, and the buildings repeatedly lost their internet connection, including over the holidays.

The permanent fix, replacing the fiber, was the university's to arrange and would take time. Residents couldn't wait for it. I installed alternative Ethernet uplinks between the buildings myself to restore connectivity, and kept them in service until the fiber was replaced.

Featured Case C: A Firmware Guide Used Nationwide

Updating Cisco switch firmware in the field is routine until it isn't: a missed step can take a switch, and everything behind it, offline. I wrote a comprehensive Cisco firmware update guide on my own initiative, and field technicians across the country used it.

A guide like that does what one person on the phone can't. Every technician follows the same steps in the same order, new technicians can learn the task without someone looking over their shoulder, and the knowledge stays with the team when people move on.

Nationwide After-Hours Escalations

On my on-call rotations, I was the escalation point for field technicians across the country, not just my own region. The right response depended on the technician and the problem, so every call started with the same question: what does this person need from me right now?

  1. Who is calling, and what is the problem?

    AssessWeigh the technician's experience against how complex and urgent the issue is. There was no fixed order.

  2. The technician can solve it with guidance

    CoachGuide the troubleshooting by phone, so they learn it for next time.

  3. It's faster or safer if I do it

    InterveneRemotely access the network equipment and work it directly.

  4. It needs a specialist or another team

    EscalateBring in engineering, or coordinate with the other on-call teams.

  5. Every path

    ThenTrack it to resolution and keep management and stakeholders updated.

Escalation decision tree. The choice depended on the technician and the issue, and could change as the call went on: when to coach, when to step in, and when to escalate.

A Personnel Case

One technician struggled with the core responsibilities of the job, with punctuality, and with professional conduct in front of university staff, and frequently took disagreements straight to upper management.

What I did
  • Held private coaching conversations
  • Listened to their concerns rather than only stating mine
  • Gave specific guidance and additional training
  • Used performance improvement plans with clear expectations
Company policy
  • Progressive discipline with formal written warnings
  • Possible offboarding after three written warnings

This case shows how I handle a difficult performance situation. How it ended isn't part of this write-up.

Financial and Operational Oversight

I didn't own a departmental budget, but spending decisions in my region went through me. I requested and compared vendor quotes, approved equipment purchases and overtime, managed inventory and spare equipment, coordinated travel, and recommended ways to cut costs.

I tracked how the region was performing on these measures:

  • SLA compliance
  • Ticket resolution time
  • First-contact resolution
  • Technician productivity
  • Customer satisfaction
  • Repeat incidents
  • Labor utilization
  • Network availability
  • Ticket backlog

I reported improvements to management in staffing coverage, travel and overtime efficiency, technician readiness, repeat calls, SLA compliance, backlog and customer satisfaction. These were qualitative and aren't independently measured here.

Still Hands-On

None of this replaced the technical work. I kept my own campuses, worked switching, wireless and fiber problems, took part in 10+ network refresh and installation projects, and worked in the data center cores the campuses depended on. That's what made the supervising work: I could tell when troubleshooting was going sideways, and my team knew I could do the job I was asking of them.
